# Skill bartering in startups: the untracked economy founders quietly rely on

**Service barter — trading professional skills directly instead of exchanging cash or equity — is a widespread but poorly documented bootstrapping strategy among early-stage startups.** No major survey from Kauffman, AngelList, or First Round Capital has ever measured its prevalence, yet nearly every authoritative bootstrapping guide acknowledges it as a legitimate tactic. The practice sits in a fascinating blind spot: common enough that the IRS has detailed rules for taxing it, practical enough that platforms have been built around it, but informal enough that Silicon Valley's top investors have published almost nothing about it. This report examines the full landscape — real examples, legal guardrails, accounting treatment, investor sentiment, advisor wisdom, community consensus, and what little data exists.

---

## Real startups that bartered their way to launch

Documented case studies of pure service-for-service barter (not equity trades) are scarce in startup literature, but several named examples illustrate the practice in action.

**Tata Tickaradze** provides the clearest MVP-stage example. In 2016, she traded her social media marketing expertise for brand identity design, graphic design, website development, copywriting, and legal and accounting work to launch her agency, To The Moon Social. "I thought to myself, could I trade my skill set to get the things I needed for my business to succeed?" she told Uncover LA. Within months she had everything she needed — no cash, no equity exchanged. The experience was so formative that she later founded **Barter & Be**, a vetted professional service exchange platform for women, launching in August 2020 with over **600 professionals** including designers, photographers, copywriters, and web developers (https://uncoverla.com/2020/08/19/barter-and-be-service-trading-platform-for-women-launches/).

**Jared Krause**, founder of the barter platform Currency (mycurrency.io), built his own ventures through serial bartering. He traded investment consulting for a graphic designer's logo work, built an app for his landlord in exchange for six months of rent, and repeatedly bartered fundraising advice to developers and product designers in exchange for dev and design services. These experiences directly shaped his startup, which aims to formalize what he had been doing informally (https://redbooth.com/blog/bartering-basics-for-startups).

**Cream City Music** in Milwaukee demonstrates barter at meaningful scale. Founder Joe Gallenberger traded guitars for roughly **$50,000 in advertising** — nearly half his annual ad budget — placing spots in a Milwaukee Pennysaver and the local edition of The Onion. He also bartered guitars for printing and plumbing services, helping the company reach **$3.5 million in revenue** with eight employees while keeping marketing costs minimal (https://money.cnn.com/2009/06/23/smallbusiness/fair_trade.fsb/index.htm).

COVID-19 accelerated formalization of startup barter. **Publicize**, a PR agency for tech startups based in Medellín, launched the **Swap Shop** in April 2020 — a free online barter marketplace where startups could exchange services during cash-strapped pandemic months (https://startupbeat.com/publicize-launches-bartering-platform-to-aid-entrepreneurs-and-startups-in-times-of-constraints/33743/). A less public but instructive case involves a SaaS founder who bartered premium calendar-tool subscriptions to a law firm in exchange for contract and legal work; the law firm subsequently became the startup's largest referral source and helped fuel their seed round (https://exitologists.com/the-forgotten-art-of-bartering/).

## The IRS treats barter dollars exactly like real dollars

Service barter between startups is fully legal under U.S. law, but its tax treatment catches many founders off guard. **The IRS treats all barter income as taxable at fair market value in the year received** — governed by IRC § 61 (gross income definition) and elaborated in IRS Publication 525 and IRS Topic No. 420 (https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc420).

The core rule is straightforward: "You must include in your income, at the time received, the fair market value of property or services you receive in bartering." If a startup trades $10,000 worth of web development for $10,000 worth of marketing services, both parties owe income tax on $10,000 — even though no cash changed hands. Barter income reported on **Schedule C** for sole proprietors is also subject to **self-employment tax** (Social Security and Medicare), creating a real cash obligation from a cashless transaction. For pre-agreed valuations, the IRS accepts mutually agreed values "unless the value can be shown to be otherwise," making written barter agreements with explicit FMV assignments essential.

Reporting requirements depend on the transaction structure. Organized **barter exchanges** must file **Form 1099-B** for all member transactions. Direct barter between two parties does not require Form 1099-B, but if a business provides services worth **$600 or more** to a contractor, Form 1099-NEC reporting may still apply — just as it would for a cash payment. Some states add additional layers: Washington State, for instance, requires both Business and Occupation tax and retail sales tax on barter transactions (https://dor.wa.gov/forms-publications/publications-subject/tax-topics/bartering-transactions-are-taxable).

**The SEC line is clear but often misunderstood.** Pure service-for-service barter does not implicate securities laws. The critical boundary is crossed when barter shifts to service-for-equity — at that point, under the Howey Test (*SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.*, 328 U.S. 293), the arrangement likely constitutes a securities sale. A contractor providing services in exchange for stock, options, SAFEs, or convertible notes has effectively purchased a security, triggering registration requirements or the need for an exemption like **Rule 701** (for compensatory equity to natural persons) or **Regulation D** (https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/common-startup-securities). Even informal promises of future equity can constitute investment contracts under Howey. The practical takeaway: **service-for-service barter avoids securities complexity entirely**, which is one of its underappreciated advantages over equity-for-services arrangements.

| Arrangement | Securities law triggered? | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|
| Service for service (pure barter) | **No** | Tax reporting; contract enforceability |
| Service for equity (stock, options) | **Yes** | Must comply with Rule 701 or Reg D |
| Service for SAFE or convertible note | **Yes** | Form D filing required |
| Service for informal future equity promise | **Likely yes** | Litigation exposure under Howey |

## GAAP accounting creates a harsh catch-22 for startups

The accounting treatment of barter transactions under US GAAP is governed primarily by **ASC 845** (Nonmonetary Transactions) and, when the exchange qualifies as a contract with a customer, by **ASC 606** (Revenue from Contracts with Customers). The rules are more restrictive than most founders expect.

Under ASC 845-10-30-1, nonmonetary exchanges should generally be recorded at **fair value** — the same basis as monetary transactions. A gain or loss is recognized on the exchange. However, three exceptions force recording at **carrying amount** (often zero for startups): when fair value is not determinable within reasonable limits, when the exchange is between entities in the same line of business facilitating customer sales, or when the transaction **lacks commercial substance** (ASC 845-10-30-3). The commercial substance test (ASC 845-10-30-4) requires that the entity's future cash flows change significantly as a result of the exchange — evaluated by whether the risk, timing, and amount of cash flows differ between what was given and received.

For startups exchanging advertising or marketing services, the rules are especially punitive. Under EITF 99-17 (codified in ASC 845-10-25), **revenue from advertising barter can only be recognized at fair value if the entity has a history of receiving cash for similar advertising from third parties within a six-month lookback window**. A startup that has never sold advertising for cash — which describes most early-stage companies — cannot establish fair value and must record at zero carrying value. As the CPA Journal noted during the dot-com era, barter transactions with "no ultimate realization in cash and no overall effect on net income" risk creating "overstated revenues and artificially inflated market capitalization."

When a barter transaction does fall within ASC 606 scope (one party is a customer obtaining outputs of the other's ordinary activities), noncash consideration must be measured at fair value at contract inception (ASC 606-10-32-21). If fair value cannot be reasonably estimated, measurement falls back to the **standalone selling price** of the promised goods or services (ASC 606-10-32-22). Deloitte's guidance cautions that "a good or service is exchanged in a barter transaction precisely because the entity has encountered difficulties in selling it at a normal retail price," questioning whether retail prices represent appropriate fair value.

Disclosure requirements under ASC 845 mandate that companies describe the nature of nonmonetary exchanges, state whether they were recorded at fair value or carrying amount, and report any gains or losses recognized. For advertising barter specifically, companies must disclose revenue and expense recognized, or if fair value is indeterminate, the volume and type of advertising exchanged. The SEC reinforced these standards through its enforcement action against **Itex Corporation** for improperly recognizing barter transaction revenue (https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/administrative-proceedings/34-45502).

## Silicon Valley's investors have been largely silent on barter

After extensive searching across Y Combinator, First Round Capital, a16z, Techstars, and 500 Startups, **no major venture capital firm or top-tier accelerator has published dedicated content recommending service barter as a strategic alternative to equity.** The concept lives at the margins of startup discourse.

The closest Y Combinator has come is investing in **Simbi** (W16 batch), a service-exchange platform using credits. YC Partner Dalton Caldwell endorsed it: "Simbi has the potential to be huge. Millions of people have useful skills others can benefit from but exchanging them almost always happens on an informal, small-scale basis" (https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/simbi/). But this validated barter as a product, not as a startup funding strategy.

**Paul Graham's "The Equity Equation"** (https://paulgraham.com/equity.html) provides the strongest implicit case for barter over equity-for-services. His formula — evaluate any equity trade using 1/(1-n) — reveals that giving equity for short-term, bounded services almost never passes the breakeven test. If bartering services or paying cash can accomplish the same goal without dilution, Graham's framework strongly favors that approach.

**Stripe's official bootstrapping guide** offers the most explicit establishment endorsement, stating: "Some startups might use creative methods — like presales, crowdfunding, or bartering services with other businesses — to fund their operations. These methods can provide necessary resources without requiring a startup to give up equity or incur debt" (https://stripe.com/resources/more/the-bootstrapping-guide-for-startups-what-entrepreneurs-need-to-know). Beyond this, First Round Review, a16z, and major accelerator blogs contain no barter-specific content.

The gap is notable but explainable. Accelerator credit programs — such as a16z Speedrun's **$5 million in partner credits** from AWS, OpenAI, Microsoft, and others — are themselves a form of institutionalized barter. Venture studios like Founders Factory and Ellenox trade operational execution (engineering, product, go-to-market) for equity stakes, formalizing the sweat-equity model. The investors' silence on peer-to-peer barter may reflect that they see it as too informal and situational to warrant strategic guidance — or that it belongs to a bootstrapping world most VCs are not primarily advising.

## Advisors agree on one thing: equity for short-term services is almost always wrong

Startup advisors and ecosystem voices show remarkable consensus that **equity should be reserved for long-term, strategic relationships** — and that bounded service needs are better handled through cash, barter, or deferred compensation.

**Tom Denison** (Startup Grind, SmartHealth Catalyzer) takes the strongest anti-barter position, arguing that "bartering services defeats the purpose of entrepreneurship" because it substitutes for the revenue needed to sustain a business. He recommends affiliate programs instead (https://www.startupgrind.com/blog/psa-for-entrepreneurs-bartering-services-is-a-bad-idea/). **Kyle Racki** (CEO of Proposify, 6,000+ paid SaaS accounts) warns from personal experience: he once accepted equity for agency services and ended up "the not-so-proud shareholder of a now defunct corporation." His advice — think like an angel investor before accepting equity — implicitly supports barter for routine services (https://medium.com/@kyleracki/6-things-agencies-should-consider-before-exchanging-services-for-equity-in-a-startup-cb81e9ca506a).

**Ron Whitney**, President and CEO of the International Reciprocal Trade Association (IRTA), offers the strongest pro-barter voice: "If your business has any degree of unused capacity, in the form of time or inventory, you should use a barter strategy." He notes that organized barter platforms provide a "built-in new market for start-up companies" and may extend credit when traditional banks won't.

**David Baxter**, a developer and startup advisor, reports that from dozens of equity-for-development cases he's observed, only **one** was mostly successful. The typical failure pattern: a developer starts enthusiastically, takes a paying client, deprioritizes the equity work, and eventually abandons the project entirely. **Hal Shelton** (SCORE mentor and angel investor) suggests an underutilized middle ground: trading services for a **loan to be paid with interest at a specific date**, providing more certainty than equity (https://www.score.org/resource/blog-post/should-i-trade-my-services-equity-a-startup). **Mike Young** (internet lawyer and angel investor) recommends hybrid packages of partial cash plus equity, warning that "a client who insists that you trade your services solely for equity likely is broke and has a bad business model that will fail" (https://mikeyounglaw.com/sweat-equity-business-ownership/).

Current benchmarks from **Carta's H1 2024 data** show median advisor equity grants at **0.13%** of fully diluted equity across all stages, and **0.25%** at pre-seed. For development services exchanged for equity, expert consensus suggests **4–10%** as the appropriate range — a steep price that further argues for barter or cash when possible.

## Community consensus: pay cash, barter if you must, avoid equity for services

Online founder communities display striking alignment on the equity-vs-barter question, with the strongest signal coming from Hacker News and Startups.com forums.

A widely discussed April 2024 Hacker News thread titled **"You won't find a technical co-founder"** (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=39902372) drew **368 points and 312 comments**. The top comment captured the community view: "In 99% of cases the value is created by the builders (technical cofounder), which instantly creates awkwardness of the founder wanting far more equity, credit, and control than their contributions warrant." The consensus: non-technical founders should learn to code, pay market rate, or demonstrate significant traction before seeking technical partners.

An earlier 2013 HN thread, **"Ask HN: Is Sweat Equity a Viable Idea?"** (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=5885437), saw well-known security expert Thomas Ptacek respond bluntly: "No. So many reasons" — citing difficulty valuing equity, adverse selection, cap table pollution, and the historical failure of equity-for-services marketplaces. A 2015 **Show HN: Barter Hack** post (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=10196053) showed grassroots demand among technologists for skill exchange, with one commenter noting: "This is perfect for the one-man-band programmers amongst us… While programmers can build great tech, we rarely have the marketing/PR knowhow."

On Startups.com, multiple Q&A threads reinforce the hierarchy. "Always aim to settle questions of payment before the work happens," advises one top answer. Workers consistently report deprioritizing equity work in favor of paying clients. The community identifies **deferred compensation** — a promise of future cash payment, convertible to equity if unpaid — as an underutilized middle ground between barter and equity grants (https://www.startups.com/questions/2087/as-a-startup-is-it-better-to-find-a-way-to-pay-for-services-i-e-design-or-trade).

The cross-platform consensus distills to a clear hierarchy: **pay cash if you can, barter services if cash is unavailable, give equity only for long-term strategic commitments**.

## The data gap is itself the most telling data point

No major entrepreneurship survey specifically tracks service barter among pre-seed or bootstrapped startups. After searching Kauffman Foundation reports, the First Round "State of Startups" survey, GEM (Global Entrepreneurship Monitor), AngelList datasets, and Carta's data, **this metric simply does not exist in published research**.

What does exist paints the surrounding landscape. The IRTA estimates **$12–14 billion** in annual global barter volume across roughly **450,000 businesses** worldwide using organized exchanges. In the U.S., combining formal barter exchange activity (~$10 billion) with estimated informal barter yields roughly **$20 billion** in total small business barter volume, according to Barter News editor Bob Meyer and IRTA data. Against the U.S. Census count of approximately 30 million businesses, formal barter exchange participation represents about **1.5%** — though informal barter is believed to be several times larger.

The bootstrapping context makes barter's relevance clear: **78%** of businesses launch with self-financing (Mercury Bank), **less than 3%** of new firms receive angel investment, and **less than 1%** receive venture capital (Kauffman Foundation). A striking **76% of entrepreneurs** report challenges with non-financial resources — exactly the gap that service barter addresses. Mercury Bank's bootstrapping guide acknowledges barter is "more common than you might think" but provides no percentages (https://mercury.com/blog/lessons-in-bootstrapping).

The absence of data is itself informative. Service barter happens overwhelmingly through informal personal networks, goes unrecorded in formal surveys, and often goes unreported on tax returns (the IRS identifies barter as a significant "tax gap issue"). Multiple advisory sources — FasterCapital, Esinli Capital, J.P. Morgan's small business guides — list bartering as a standard bootstrapping tactic alongside side hustles and revenue-first models, suggesting it is broadly recognized even if never quantified. The practice exists in what might be called the **informal economy of founder resourcefulness** — real, prevalent, and nearly invisible to researchers.

## Conclusion

Service barter occupies a paradoxical position in the startup ecosystem: universally acknowledged, rarely studied, and never formally recommended by the institutions that shape startup strategy. The practice offers genuine advantages — preserving both cash and equity, avoiding securities law complexity, and converting idle capacity into tangible resources. But it comes with real obligations (IRS taxation at fair market value, GAAP recording challenges, contract enforceability risks) and real limitations (the "double coincidence of wants" problem, the inability to build revenue track records investors want to see, and quality control difficulties).

The most actionable insight from this research is that **the startup community's resistance is to equity-for-services, not to barter itself**. Founders who frame service exchange as an alternative to equity dilution — rather than an alternative to revenue — align with both advisor wisdom and community consensus. For bounded, one-time needs like logo design, initial legal work, or MVP photography, barter is a rational strategy. For ongoing technical development or strategic advisory, equity or cash remains more appropriate. The founders who use barter most effectively treat it not as a permanent operating model but as a **bridge tool during the specific window between idea and first revenue** — a window where, for the vast majority of startups, neither institutional capital nor meaningful cash flow exists.
